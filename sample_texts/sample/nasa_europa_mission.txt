NASA's Europa Clipper spacecraft has begun returning its first science data after settling into the outer solar system, the agency said Tuesday, marking a key milestone in the hunt for conditions that could support life beyond Earth. The probe, the largest planetary spacecraft NASA has ever built, is on a years-long journey toward Jupiter's icy moon Europa, which scientists believe hides a vast saltwater ocean beneath its frozen crust. Mission managers confirmed that all nine science instruments are now operating as expected. "Everything we are seeing tells us the spacecraft is healthy and ready for the science campaign ahead," said the mission's project scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Europa Clipper launched in October aboard a heavy-lift rocket and will use gravity assists from Mars and Earth to reach the Jupiter system. Once it arrives, the spacecraft will make dozens of close flybys of Europa, dipping as low as 16 miles above the surface. Instruments aboard include cameras, an ice-penetrating radar, and tools to sample the thin wisps of gas around the moon. Scientists hope the radar can confirm the depth of the suspected ocean and whether it touches a rocky seafloor, a setup that could provide the chemistry needed for life. The moon is bathed in intense radiation from Jupiter, so the craft's electronics are shielded inside a thick metal vault. Researchers caution that the mission is not designed to detect life directly, only to assess whether Europa is habitable. The total cost of the mission is about 5 billion dollars. Data from the flybys will take years to analyze. The team plans to share early images with the public later this year.
